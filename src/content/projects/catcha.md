          The release mechanism itself was relatively straightforward: I just need enough vertical height for the toy to build up speed as it rolled down the track. The more challenging part was designing a handle that a cat could actually operate. 
          <br><br>A successful initiation involved two steps: <b>attracting the cat’s attention enough to encourage physical interaction, and allowing enough movement to trigger the internal release mechanism.</b>

          <br><br>1. Fluffy mouse: store-bought, bite-sized

  - title: Designing the mechanism
    accent: "#525252"
    blocks:
      - html: | 
          With the bait selected, I built cardboard prototypes to test different mechanisms. 
          <br><br>Prototype v1 was inspired by traditional gumball machines, using a rotating handle that is intuitive for humans to operate with just two fingers. While the mechanism worked perfectly for humans, the compartment wheel required too much force for Xuxu to rotate.
          <br><br>Prototype v2 replaced the rotational motion with a pull-down mechanism, which was more achievable with a cat’s paws. This iteration successfully allowed Xuxu to trigger the release mechanism independently.

  - title: User testing & reflections
    accent: "#525252"
    blocks:
      - html: |
          During the initial testing of the machine, I noticed that Xuxu had lost interest in the paper octopus. Because I had used the same bait during the initial testing phase and then attached it to the machine, pulling on the octopus did not consistently result in a toy being released. This inconsistency made it difficult for Xuxu to establish the causal relationship between <b>pulling the bait and activating the machine</b>.
          <br><br>I replaced the octopus with a feather, which successfully piqued Xuxu’s interest again. With the new bait, he was able to learn the interaction and operate the machine independently.

  - title: Takeaways
    variant: circle
    blocks:
      - html: |
          Individual cats can have very different ways of learning. Cats that are naturally rewarded by play (e.g. Xuxu) may quickly learn to operate a machine like Catcha, while cats that are more strongly rewarded by food, or cats that are physically challenged, may have little incentive to engage with it in the first place.
          <br><br>This reinforced an insight that applies beyond animal-centered design: users do not share the same motivations, preferences, or learning curves. Just as we account for individual differences when designing for humans, designing for animals requires us to understand what each user finds rewarding and design the interaction around it.
